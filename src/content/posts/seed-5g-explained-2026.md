Here's a fact that would have been headline news five years ago and is a footnote today: **every single phone in our 100-device catalog supports 5G** — from the $199 [Redmi Note 14](/product/redmi-note-14) to the $1,999 [iPhone Duo](/product/iphone-duo). 5G went from premium differentiator to table stakes. This guide explains what 5G actually is, why you can stop using it as a buying criterion, and the connectivity details that *do* still matter.

## What 5G actually is

5G is the fifth generation of cellular network technology — the successor to 4G LTE. In practical terms it delivers three things:

1. **Higher peak speeds** — real-world 5G typically delivers 100–500 Mbps down, versus 20–100 Mbps on LTE. Enough for 4K streaming, instant cloud photo backup, and tethering a laptop without thinking about it.
2. **Lower latency** — response times roughly halved versus 4G, which matters for gaming, video calls, and real-time apps.
3. **More capacity** — networks handle crowded stadiums and city centers better, so your phone works when thousands of others are competing for signal.

That's the honest, non-marketing version. The "download a movie in seconds" demos were real but irrelevant — nobody's bottleneck was ever download speed for long.

## The two flavors: sub-6 and mmWave

5G runs on two very different kinds of spectrum, and the distinction explains most real-world disappointment with 5G:

- **Sub-6 GHz ("low/mid-band"):** long range, penetrates buildings, modest speed boost over 4G. This is the 5G almost everyone actually uses, and it's genuinely good — T-Mobile's mid-band network in the US and similar deployments worldwide deliver the 100–500 Mbps experience above.
- **mmWave:** extremely fast (gigabits) but with a range measured in hundreds of meters and poor wall penetration. It's deployed in select stadiums, airports, and downtown cores. Your phone may support it; you'll rarely connect to it.

When a phone's spec sheet says "5G: Yes" — as every phone in our catalog does — it almost always means sub-6. mmWave support is an extra, region-specific detail that spec databases (including ours) generally don't track per model.

## Why 5G stopped being a buying factor

Three reasons:

**1. Universal support.** In 2026, 5G modems are integrated into every mobile chip from the Helio G99 Ultra ([Redmi Note 14](/product/redmi-note-14), $199) to the Apple A20 Pro ([iPhone 18 Pro](/product/iphone-18-pro), $1,199) to the Snapdragon 8 Elite Gen 5 ([Galaxy S26 Ultra](/product/galaxy-s26-ultra), $1,300). There is no 4G-only phone left in the current market worth buying. The "5G" row in our comparison tables is "Yes" across the board — it decides nothing.

**2. The network matters more than the phone.** Your 5G experience is determined by your carrier's local deployment, not your handset. A $299 [POCO X7 Pro](/product/poco-x7-pro) on a good mid-band network will feel faster than a $1,300 Ultra on a congested one.

**3. The real-world gains plateaued.** For messaging, maps, streaming, and browsing — 95% of phone use — good LTE was already sufficient. 5G's headroom matters for tethering, cloud gaming, and heavy uploaders, but it's not the day-to-day revolution the launch ads promised.

## What to check instead of "does it have 5G"

Since the answer is always yes, spend your connectivity attention here:

- **Carrier band compatibility (imports especially).** This is the one genuine trap: a phone built for China or Europe may lack the specific 5G bands your carrier uses, dropping you to LTE or worse. If you're importing a [Xiaomi](/product/xiaomi-16), [vivo](/product/vivo-x200-pro), or [OPPO](/product/oppo-find-x8-pro) device (many list "Price TBA" in USD precisely because they're not officially sold stateside), verify band support with your carrier before buying. We don't track per-band data in our catalog — check the manufacturer's regional spec page.
- **Wi-Fi standard.** Wi-Fi 6E/7 matters more than 5G for most people, since phones spend most of their lives on Wi-Fi. Flagships in 2026 generally support the latest standards; budget phones may not.
- **Dual SIM / eSIM.** Dual-SIM (physical + eSIM or dual eSIM) is genuinely useful for travelers and work/personal splits. Support varies more than 5G does — check the specific model.
- **Satellite messaging.** Apple's Emergency SOS via satellite and similar features are the actual new connectivity frontier — worth more attention than another 100 Mbps of peak 5G.

## 5G and battery life: the footnote that matters

Early 5G phones had noticeably worse battery life with 5G enabled. In 2026, integrated modems and smarter network switching have mostly solved this — but 5G still draws more power than LTE when the signal is weak, because the radio works harder. If you're squeezing a travel day out of a [Pixel 10a](/product/pixel-10a) (5,100mAh) or any phone, dropping to LTE in a poor-signal area remains a legitimate battery-saving trick.

## The bottom line

5G in 2026 is like power steering: universal, expected, and not something you shop for. Every phone from $199 to $1,999 has it. Judge phones on the things that still differ — camera systems, battery capacity and charging speed, chip longevity, display quality, and price — and let 5G take care of itself. ## 5G Advanced and what's next

The industry is already moving past today's 5G toward **5G Advanced** (sometimes called 5.5G): better uplink speeds, lower latency, improved efficiency, and early support for things like precise positioning and ambient IoT. In practice, for phone buyers, it's an evolution, not a revolution — your 2026 phone's modem will handle it via software where carriers deploy it, and none of it changes what phone you should buy.

Beyond that sits 6G research, targeting commercial deployment around 2030. It promises sensing-plus-communication (your phone as a radar, essentially) and terabit ambitions — interesting for engineers, irrelevant for your 2026 purchase decision. Buy for the next 3–4 years, not the next decade.

## 5G FAQ

**Do I need a special plan for 5G?** Generally no — most carriers include 5G access in standard plans now, though some budget MVNOs still gate it behind higher tiers. Check your plan, not your phone.

**Does 5G cost extra battery?** Slightly more than LTE in weak signal, roughly equal in good signal on modern chips. The 2nm and 3nm modems in 2026 phones ([iPhone 18 Pro](/product/iphone-18-pro), [Galaxy S26 Ultra](/product/galaxy-s26-ultra), [Pixel 10 Pro](/product/pixel-10-pro)) are far more efficient than the first-generation 5G modems that gave the technology its battery-hungry reputation.

**Is 4G LTE going away?** Not soon. Carriers are refarming some LTE spectrum for 5G, but LTE remains the fallback everywhere and will for years. A 5G phone uses LTE automatically whenever 5G is unavailable — you never need to think about it.

**Should I care about mmWave support?** Only if you spend time in the specific venues where it's deployed (stadiums, airports, dense downtown). For everyone else, sub-6 5G is the real network, and every 2026 phone supports it.

**What about a phone without 5G?** Don't buy one new in 2026. There are effectively none left in the current market, and carriers will eventually sunset older networks. Any new phone you buy today will have 5G — which is rather the point of this guide.
